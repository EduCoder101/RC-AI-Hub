Riff on ideas, draft something rough, pressure-test your thinking. It works at the speed of conversation.

What is Gemini?

A conversation with AI, nothing more complicated than that

Gemini is Google's AI assistant, and it is available to you right now through your Roseville College Google Workspace account. There is nothing to install, no special software to learn, and no training required before you begin.

Using Gemini is like having a conversation. You type a question or an instruction, and it responds. If the answer is not quite right, you refine your request, just as you would if you were talking to a colleague. If you can write an email, you can use Gemini.

  • It works through a simple chat interface in your browser
  • Your conversations are private to your Workspace account
  • There is no wrong way to start. Just type what you need
  • You can ask follow-up questions to shape and refine the response
How to access

Go to gemini.google.com and sign in with your Roseville College Google account. That's it.

What makes a great prompt?

Most people’s first prompts are too vague to produce anything useful. The examples below show what changes when you get specific. Each one represents a different way to use Gemini. Click through to see the difference.

× Vague
Write me a parent email about camp.
✓ Specific
You are an experienced Year 9 English teacher at an independent girls’ school in Sydney. Write a warm, professional email to parents informing them about our upcoming Year 9 camp to the Blue Mountains. The camp runs 12–14 August, costs $320, and requires permission forms back by 28 July. The tone should be enthusiastic but clear. End with a reminder that questions can be directed to the Year 9 coordinator.
Why this works
Role
“You are an experienced Year 9 English teacher at an independent girls’ school in Sydney.”
Giving the AI a role shapes its vocabulary, tone, and assumptions. “Teacher at an independent girls’ school in Sydney” produces very different language to just “teacher”.
Context
“…informing them about our upcoming Year 9 camp to the Blue Mountains”
Context tells the AI what situation it’s working in. The more specific the scenario, the more relevant the output.
Task
“Write a warm, professional email to parents…”
The actual instruction. Note it specifies both format (email) and tone (warm, professional) rather than leaving either to chance.
Constraints
“The camp runs 12–14 August… End with a reminder that questions can be directed to the Year 9 coordinator.”
Constraints pin down the details and boundaries. Dates, costs, deadlines, and structural requirements all belong here.
× Vague
Give me ideas for a Year 10 history unit.
✓ Specific
I’m planning a Year 10 Modern History unit on civil rights movements. I want students to draw parallels between the American civil rights movement and the Australian Indigenous rights movement. I’m torn between a comparative essay and a structured source analysis as the final assessment. My class has 28 students, most of whom struggle with extended writing but engage well with primary sources. Talk me through the trade-offs of each assessment type for this cohort, and suggest which might better demonstrate their understanding.
Why this works
A real dilemma
“I’m torn between a comparative essay and a structured source analysis”
You’re not asking for generic ideas. You’re bringing a specific decision you need help thinking through.
Student context
“most of whom struggle with extended writing but engage well with primary sources”
This changes the answer completely. Without it, the AI is guessing about your students.
Asking for reasoning
“Talk me through the trade-offs”
You’re asking the AI to think with you, not just produce something. This is Gemini as a thinking partner.
Decision framing
“suggest which might better demonstrate their understanding”
The conversation is anchored to a clear purpose: which option best serves your students.
× Vague
Make this worksheet easier for my lower ability students.
✓ Specific
I have a Year 8 Science worksheet on chemical reactions that my extension students handled well, but my core class is struggling. The main barriers are the abstract vocabulary (reactant, product, exothermic) and the jump from the diagram to the written explanation. The learning goal is for students to identify the signs of a chemical reaction and distinguish them from physical changes. Rewrite this worksheet with: concrete, everyday examples before introducing technical terms; a guided scaffold that bridges the diagram to the written response; and a word bank with simple definitions.[worksheet pasted below]
Why this works
Named the barrier
“the abstract vocabulary… and the jump from the diagram to the written explanation”
Not “make it easier”. You’ve diagnosed what’s actually causing students to struggle.
Preserved the goal
“identify the signs of a chemical reaction and distinguish them from physical changes”
Simpler does not mean dumbed down. The learning goal stays the same; the path to it changes.
Directed the scaffolding
“concrete examples before technical terms; a guided scaffold; a word bank”
You’re specifying the type of support, not just asking for “easier”.
Comparison context
“my extension students handled well, but my core class is struggling”
This tells the AI the level shift needed, not just that it should be simpler.
× Vague
Here are my class results. What do you think?
✓ Specific
Below are the results from my Year 11 Biology half-yearly, broken down by question. Questions 1–5 tested recall, 6–10 tested application, and 11–15 tested analysis and evaluation. Identify which skill level the class struggled with most. Group students into three tiers based on their performance profile (not just total score): those who need support with foundational knowledge, those who can recall but struggle to apply, and those who are ready for extension. For each group, suggest one targeted revision activity I could run next week.[results pasted below]
Why this works
Structured the data
“Questions 1–5 tested recall, 6–10 tested application, 11–15 tested analysis”
You’ve told the AI what the numbers mean, not just handed over raw data.
Asked for patterns
“Identify which skill level the class struggled with most”
You don’t want averages or summaries. You want insight you can act on.
Defined useful groupings
“three tiers based on their performance profile (not just total score)”
You’re telling the AI how you actually think about differentiation.
Connected to action
“suggest one targeted revision activity I could run next week”
Analysis is only useful if it leads somewhere practical.
× Vague
Create a rubric for a Year 9 persuasive essay.
✓ Specific
I need a complete assessment task for Year 9 English: a persuasive speech on whether social media does more harm than good for teenagers. Create the following as one package: (1) A student-facing task sheet with clear instructions, word count (600–800 words), and a checklist of what to include. (2) A marking rubric with four criteria (thesis and argument structure, use of evidence, persuasive techniques, language and conventions) across four performance levels, with specific descriptors for each cell. (3) Two annotated sample responses: one at a C-grade level and one at an A-grade level, with brief margin notes explaining why each sits at that level. Align everything to NSW English syllabus outcome EN5-1A.
Why this works
Defined the deliverables
“a student-facing task sheet… a marking rubric… two annotated sample responses”
You’re not asking for one thing. You’re briefing a complete, ready-to-use package.
Set precise parameters
“four criteria… across four performance levels… with specific descriptors for each cell”
The AI knows exactly what the rubric grid should look like.
Asked for calibration
“one at a C-grade level and one at an A-grade level, with margin notes”
Sample responses at different grades are what make a resource actually usable for students and moderators.
Anchored to standards
“Align everything to NSW English syllabus outcome EN5-1A”
The output is accountable to real curriculum requirements, not just generic quality.
