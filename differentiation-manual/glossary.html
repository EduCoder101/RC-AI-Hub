Step 3a: Differentiate Content Strategies

Content differentiation ensures equitable access to learning by adjusting how students encounter information.

Tiered Texts & Resource Materials → When reading levels vary

+

What it is: Provide reading materials at different complexity and readability levels so students can engage with the same overarching theme regardless of their current literacy skills.

When to use: When core content involves significant reading and you have students at varied reading levels.

Link: Full details in Step 3a →

Multi-Modal Presentations → For diverse learning profiles

+

What it is: Deliver information through multiple modes: auditory, visual, and tactile/kinaesthetic.

When to use: When students have varied learning profiles or when content is complex and benefits from multiple representations.

Link: Full details in Step 3a →

Curriculum Compacting → When students have mastered content

+

What it is: Three-stage process: identify mastered material, eliminate redundant practice, replace with meaningful extensions.

When to use: When pre-assessment reveals students have already mastered portions of the content.

Link: Full details in Step 3a →

Digest of Key Ideas → When students are overwhelmed

+

What it is: One- or two-page capsule of essential questions, vocabulary, and concepts.

When to use: When students are overwhelmed by lengthy texts or complex materials.

Link: Full details in Step 3a →

Highlighted Print Materials → For lengthy essential texts

+

What it is: Highlight critical passages so struggling readers can focus energy on essential portions.

When to use: When lengthy text contains essential content but not every paragraph is equally important.

Link: Full details in Step 3a →

Support Systems (Buddies, Mentors, Technology) → When content is challenging

+

What it is: Use reading buddies, adult mentors, or audio/video recordings to make complex content accessible.

When to use: When content is challenging but essential and students need support to access it.

Link: Full details in Step 3a →

Visual Supports (Glossaries, Mind Maps, Flashcards) → For vocabulary-heavy content

+

What it is: Visual tools that help students organise, remember, and connect content.

When to use: When content is vocabulary-heavy, conceptually dense, or requires seeing relationships between ideas.

Link: Full details in Step 3a →

Step 3b: Differentiate Process Strategies

Process differentiation adjusts how students make sense of and internalise ideas.

Tiered Activities → When readiness levels vary

+

What it is: Multiple versions of a task focusing on the same essential understanding but varying in complexity and support.

When to use: When students have varied readiness levels for a particular skill or concept.

Link: Full details in Step 3b →

Flexible Grouping → Use constantly - the heart of differentiation

+

What it is: Move students between various group configurations based on the task at hand.

When to use: Constantly. The "heart" of differentiation. Prevents permanent tracking.

Link: Full details in Step 3b →

Learning Centres/Stations → For independent practice

+

What it is: Designated classroom areas where students rotate through different activities.

When to use: When you want students to practice skills or explore concepts independently or in small groups.

Link: Full details in Step 3b →

Cubing → For multi-perspective exploration

+

What it is: Analyse a topic from six perspectives (describe, compare, associate, analyse, apply, argue).

When to use: When you want students to explore a concept from multiple angles.

Link: Full details in Step 3b →

Jigsaw → When topic has distinct parts

+

What it is: Students become "experts" in one aspect, then teach peers in home groups.

When to use: When a topic has multiple distinct parts that can be learned somewhat independently.

Link: Full details in Step 3b →

Learning Contracts → For independent, ready students

+

What it is: Formal agreements where teacher grants freedom in exchange for completing individualised components.

When to use: When students are ready for more independence and self-direction.

Link: Full details in Step 3b →

Interactive Journals → For ongoing reflection

+

What it is: Journals with varied writing prompts based on readiness level.

When to use: When you want students to process and personalise content through writing.

Link: Full details in Step 3b →

Anchor Activities → Always - manages different pace

+

What it is: Self-directed tasks students move to automatically upon completing assigned work.

When to use: Always. Manages "ragged time" when students finish at different rates.

Link: Full details in Step 3b →

Step 3c: Differentiate Product Strategies

Product differentiation offers diverse ways for students to demonstrate mastery.

Choice Boards & Menus → To give student agency

+

What it is: Grids or menus of activity options allowing students to select how they demonstrate learning.

When to use: When you want to give students agency in demonstrating learning.

Link: Full details in Step 3c →

RAFT (Role, Audience, Format, Topic) → For authentic application

+

What it is: Students take on a role, write for an audience, in a format, about a topic.

When to use: When you want students to apply content knowledge in creative, authentic ways.

Link: Full details in Step 3c →

Negotiated Criteria → To build student ownership

+

What it is: Collaborate with students to determine core requirements while allowing personal quality goals.

When to use: When you want to build student ownership while maintaining rigour.

Link: Full details in Step 3c →

Tiered Assessments → When readiness varies significantly

+

What it is: Multiple versions of summative tasks that assess the same standards but vary in complexity.

When to use: When students have significantly varied readiness levels.

Link: Full details in Step 3c →

Performance & Presentation Products → For demonstrable skills

+

What it is: Students demonstrate learning through presenting, teaching, demonstrating, or performing.

When to use: When learning involves demonstrable skills or when students excel at oral communication.

Link: Full details in Step 3c →

Creative & Artistic Products → For emotional/interpretive content

+

What it is: Students demonstrate understanding through art, design, storytelling, or creative expression.

When to use: When content has thematic dimensions or when students need alternative ways to show understanding.

Link: Full details in Step 3c →

Collaborative Group Products → When collaboration is the goal

+

What it is: Products requiring genuine collaboration that no individual could create alone.

When to use: When learning goals include collaboration or when task scope requires distributed effort.

Link: Full details in Step 3c →

Step 6: Scaffolding Strategies

Scaffolds provide temporary support to help students access challenge.

Graphic Organisers → When students struggle to organise

+

What it is: Visual frameworks that help students organise thinking and see relationships between ideas.

When to use: When students understand concepts but struggle to organise or connect ideas.

Link: Full details in Step 6 →

Sentence Starters & Thinking Stems → For writing and discussion

+

What it is: Phrases that help students begin thinking, writing, or discussion.

When to use: For writing tasks, discussions, or when students need models for academic language.

Link: Full details in Step 6 →

Chunking & Step-by-Step Instructions → For complex multi-step tasks

+

What it is: Breaking complex tasks into smaller, manageable steps.

When to use: When students understand content but struggle with multi-step processes.

Link: Full details in Step 6 →

Models & Exemplars → When introducing new formats

+

What it is: Showing students what quality work looks like through concrete examples.

When to use: When introducing new formats, genres, or quality standards.

Link: Full details in Step 6 →

Thinking Partners & Collaboration → When dialogue deepens understanding

+

What it is: Strategic pairing so students support each other's thinking.

When to use: When students would benefit from talking through ideas or multiple perspectives.

Link: Full details in Step 6 →

Worked Examples & Think-Alouds → For new skills or problem types

+

What it is: Demonstrating the thinking process, not just the final answer.

When to use: When introducing new skills or problem types.

Link: Full details in Step 6 →

Step 7: Formative Assessment Strategies

Formative assessment provides ongoing data to guide differentiation decisions.

Exit Cards/Tickets → End of most lessons

+

What it is: Brief written responses at end of lesson providing snapshot data.

When to use: End of most lessons, particularly after introducing new concepts.

Link: Full details in Step 7 →

QuickWrites → Mid-lesson checks

+

What it is: Brief, timed writing (2-3 minutes) synthesising learning.

When to use: Mid-lesson to check understanding before moving on.

Link: Full details in Step 7 →

Think-Pair-Share & Structured Discussion → Throughout lessons

+

What it is: Think individually, discuss with partner, share with class while teacher observes.

When to use: Throughout lessons to check understanding and process information.

Link: Full details in Step 7 →

Dry Erase Boards/Immediate Response → For quick checks

+

What it is: Students show answers simultaneously so you can immediately see who understands.

When to use: For quick checks during instruction or practising skills.

Link: Full details in Step 7 →

Varying Questions by Readiness → During whole-class discussion

+

What it is: Target different questions to different students during discussion based on their readiness.

When to use: During whole-class discussions and questioning.

Link: Full details in Step 7 →

S-O-S Summary (Statement-Opinion-Support) → For low-stakes argumentation

+

What it is: Brief response: state opinion on statement, then support with evidence.

When to use: When you want students to practice argumentation in a low-stakes format.

Link: Full details in Step 7 →
