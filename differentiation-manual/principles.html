The Gap Between Knowing and Doing

Ask any teacher what good differentiation looks like and they'll describe it perfectly: tasks that meet students where they are, that build toward the same learning intention through different pathways. But under time pressure, differentiation often defaults to simpler versions — easier questions for some, harder questions for others.

This isn't a failure of knowledge — it's a failure of systems. Teachers need frameworks and tools that make sophisticated differentiation achievable, not just aspirational.

Research: Suprayogi, Valcke & Godwin (2017) found teachers frequently default to "different amounts" rather than "different approaches." Smale-Jacobse et al. (2019) confirmed that explicit frameworks significantly help teachers move beyond intuitive but limited practices.

The Continuum Model

Every differentiated lesson has a centre: the core learning intention all students work toward. The continuum builds outward from that centre in both directions.

Foundations strategies help students build prerequisite skills and confidence to access the core. Extension strategies move students who've grasped the core toward abstraction, transfer, and deeper connections.

The key insight: both directions serve the same learning intention. Foundations work isn't "easy work" — it's building toward the same goal through a different pathway. Extension work isn't "extra work" — it's deepening the same understanding.

Example: In Year 5 fractions, foundations work involves manipulatives and visual models. Extension work involves applying fractions to unfamiliar real-world contexts or exploring their relationship with decimals and percentages. Same learning intention, different pathways.

What Foundations Work Actually Looks Like

Effective foundations strategies include: simplifying language without simplifying concepts, providing visual and concrete supports, breaking tasks into manageable steps, offering sentence starters, and creating bilingual bridges for EALD students.

Notice what's missing: fewer questions, shorter texts, less work. Those are quantitative adjustments. Foundations strategies are qualitative — they change the nature of the support, not the amount of the task.

Research: Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976) defined scaffolding as temporary support that enables students to complete tasks they couldn't manage independently — with the goal of removing the support as competence develops.

Example: A student struggling with written expression doesn't need fewer sentences. They need sentence starters, a graphic organiser, and a model. Same learning intention, different pathway.

What Extension Work Actually Looks Like

"She's finished early — give her the extension sheet." If extension work is just more problems at the same level, we're rewarding speed with punishment.

True extension moves students along specific dimensions: from concrete to abstract, from structured to open-ended, from familiar contexts to unfamiliar ones, from single perspectives to multiple. These come from Tomlinson's Equaliser — each dimension is a purposeful movement, not just "harder."

Research: Van Tassel-Baska & Stambaugh (2006) identify four dimensions of meaningful extension: depth, complexity, abstractness, and pacing. Boaler (2008) warns that when extension becomes permanent tracking with different expectations, it entrenches inequality.

Example: A student who's mastered persuasive structure doesn't need another essay. They could analyse how persuasive techniques differ across advertising, politics, and science — applying the same skill across unfamiliar contexts.

The Continuum Is Not a Label

The biggest risk with any differentiation model is it becomes fixed ability grouping. "She's a foundations student" — the moment the continuum labels the child instead of the strategy, it becomes harmful.

The continuum describes where a strategy helps most for a specific learning intention at a specific point in time. A student might need foundations support in fractions and extension in creative writing — in the same week.

Research: Dweck (2006) shows that "easier work" signals fixed ability, while "different support toward the same goal" signals growth. Boaler (2008) found that flexible pathways with common high-level goals benefit all students; permanent grouping entrenches inequality.

In practice: Use the continuum to choose strategies, not to sort students. Keep groups moving.

Anticipatory Differentiation

Use AI to interrogate your lessons from different student perspectives before you deliver them. Ask: What could be misconstrued? Where are the gaps? What's unclear? Where are likely misconceptions?

Why this matters: Catching potential barriers before instruction begins prevents students from struggling unnecessarily. Proactive differentiation is more effective and less exhausting than reactive differentiation.

Research: Tomlinson (2014) defines effective differentiation as proactive rather than reactive — using class profiles and pre-assessment data to plan for diversity from the outset, ensuring lessons have built-in flexibility before they are delivered.

Connected strategies: Stress-Test Your Goals (Step 1), Predictive Misconception Mapping (Step 3)

Responsive Differentiation

Effective differentiation responds to evidence — your knowledge of students' readiness, interests, and learning profiles combined with ongoing formative assessment data. Build deidentified class profiles to capture what you know, and use quick checks, exit cards, and observations to update that picture continuously.

Why this matters: Generic differentiation helps no one. When you differentiate based on actual student data, you create materials that genuinely meet learners where they are.

Research: Hattie's (2012) meta-analyses identify formative assessment (d=0.48) as one of the most powerful influences on achievement when used to inform instruction. Tomlinson (2014) emphasises that differentiation should be data-driven, with ongoing assessment guiding responsive adjustments.

Connected strategies: Create a Pre-Assessment (Step 2), Build a Class Profile (Step 2), Exit Cards (Step 7)

Collaborative Differentiation

For Tier 2 and especially Tier 3 students, differentiation works best when you consult with inclusive learning specialists who know the students well and can guide targeted interventions. AI can help you create materials, but specialists provide the pedagogical insight that ensures those materials work for students with complex needs.

Why this matters: Complex learning needs require expert support. Collaboration between classroom teachers and specialists produces more effective differentiated practices than either working alone.

Research: Research on Multi-Tiered Systems of Support (MTSS) consistently shows that co-construction of lessons with inclusive learning specialists is associated with more effective differentiation. Schools using collaborative team models report higher teacher efficacy and more equitable outcomes (Goddard, Goddard & Kim, 2015).

Connected strategies: Neurodiversity-Responsive Content Design (Step 3), EALD Translation Bridge (Step 6)

Differentiation is Qualitative

Not just "more work" or "less work"

Click to learn more →

Quality Over Quantity

Differentiation isn't about giving advanced students more problems or struggling students fewer. It's about adjusting the nature and complexity of the work. Foundations strategies change the nature of the support; extension strategies change the nature of the challenge. Neither is about volume.

Why this matters: Quantitative differentiation (three problems vs ten problems) sends the message that learning is about volume. Qualitative differentiation honours that all students deserve appropriately challenging, meaningful work — just through different pathways.

Research: Tomlinson (2001, 2014) distinguishes qualitative from quantitative differentiation and emphasises that adjustments should change the nature of the task, not just the amount. This aligns with the continuum model where movement is along dimensions of complexity, abstraction, and transfer.

Connected strategies: The Equaliser (Step 5) — all 8 dimensions describe qualitative adjustments

Building Self-Reliant Learners

Effective differentiation helps students become aware of their own learning needs and develop the agency to meet them. The goal isn't perpetual dependence on teacher-created supports — it's helping students recognise when they need different approaches and how to access them.

Why this matters: The most powerful differentiation is the kind students eventually do for themselves. When students can self-assess, choose appropriate challenges, and seek the right kind of help, they become independent learners.

Research: Hattie's meta-analyses identify "assessment-capable learners" (d=1.33) as one of the most powerful influences on achievement. When students are taught to understand their own learning profiles and make choices, they move from passive recipients to active managers of their own progress.

Connected strategies: Learning Contracts (Step 3), Negotiated Criteria (Step 3), Peer Assessment Checklists (Step 7)

Differentiation for All Students

Differentiation isn't remediation in disguise. Every student benefits from instruction that matches their readiness, interests, and learning profile — including students working above grade level, students in the middle who are often overlooked, and students with specific learning profiles.

Why this matters: When we think of differentiation as only for students who struggle, we miss opportunities to challenge and engage every learner. The continuum makes this visible: there are strategies to the left AND right of centre, because all students deserve a pathway that fits.

Research: Tomlinson (2014) and Subban (2006) both emphasise that differentiation benefits all students when implemented as a whole-class approach rather than targeted intervention.

Connected strategies: Curriculum Compacting (Step 3), Anchor Activities (Step 3), Choice Boards (Step 3)

Content Differentiation Creates Equity

If a student can't access the content because it's too complex, too abstract, or presented in an inaccessible format, no amount of process or product differentiation will help. Content access is the foundation of equitable learning.

Why this matters: This is why foundations strategies matter so much — they ensure every student can reach the core learning intention. The foundations column on the continuum is rich with content access strategies because that's where the equity work begins.

Research: Tomlinson (2014) and Subban (2006) emphasise that content differentiation is the most powerful lever for ensuring equitable access to learning.

Connected strategies: Tiered Texts (Step 3), Digest of Key Ideas (Step 3), EALD Translation Bridge (Step 6), Academic Language Scaffolds (Step 6)

AI as Your Differentiation Partner

AI tools don't create more work — they create more capacity. They handle the production work (creating multiple versions, generating scaffolds, designing tiered tasks) so you can focus on the professional judgement work (deciding what students need, reviewing materials, making responsive adjustments).

Why this matters: Without AI, creating three to five versions of quality learning materials is unsustainable. With AI, you can create bespoke materials at scale, making genuine personalisation feasible in a classroom of 25+ students.

Research: Emerging research highlights AI as a "pedagogical partner" that addresses the long-standing challenge of individualised instruction at scale — shifting from administrative efficiency tool to capacity builder that prevents teacher burnout.
