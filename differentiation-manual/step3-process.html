Process: Where the Cognitive Work Happens

Process encompasses the activities and cognitive routines students use to internalize ideas. Once students can access content (Step 3a), process differentiation lets you be intentional about where students do the cognitive lifting.

This is where you can control the complexity of thinking, adjust the amount of scaffolding, and ensure students are working in their Zone of Proximal Development—challenged but not overwhelmed.

Process Differentiation Strategies

2

Tiered Activities

▼

Design multiple versions of a task that focus on the same essential understanding but vary in complexity, abstraction, and required support. All students work toward the same learning goals, but the pathway differs based on readiness.

When to use: When students have varied readiness levels for a particular skill or concept. Tiering ensures everyone is appropriately challenged—working in their Zone of Proximal Development, not too easy, not impossibly hard.
Example in practice: All students analyse a character's motivation, but:
  • Tier 1: Provided with specific text evidence and guided questions
  • Tier 2: Given general prompts and must find their own evidence
  • Tier 3: Compare character motivations across multiple texts and evaluate how context shapes motivation

AI Prompts:

Create Tiered Versions of an Activity

Here's an activity I use with students: [describe activity]

My KUDs for this activity are: [paste KUDs]

Create three tiered versions:

  • Tier 1 (Support): More structured, step-by-step guidance, concrete examples. Students still do meaningful thinking, but with scaffolding.
  • Tier 2 (Core): Appropriate challenge for on-level students.
  • Tier 3 (Extension): More open-ended, requires abstract thinking, asks students to synthesise or evaluate rather than just understand.

All three tiers must address the same essential understanding—vary the complexity, not the goal.

Tier by Adjusting Cognitive Demand

Take this task: [describe task]

Create three versions that adjust the cognitive demand:

  • Version 1: Students recall and understand (Bloom's: Remember/Understand)
  • Version 2: Students apply and analyse (Bloom's: Apply/Analyse)
  • Version 3: Students evaluate and create (Bloom's: Evaluate/Create)

Ensure all versions require genuine thinking—Version 1 isn't "easier," it's addressing a different level of cognitive complexity.

4

Flexible Grouping

▼

Move students between various group configurations based on the task at hand. Sometimes group by readiness (homogeneous), sometimes by interest (mixed), sometimes randomly. The key word is flexible—groups change frequently to prevent tracking.

When to use: Constantly. Flexible grouping is considered the "heart" of differentiation. It allows you to target instruction to specific needs while ensuring students don't get stuck in permanent ability groups.
Example in practice: Monday, group by readiness for targeted maths instruction. Wednesday, group by interest for a research project. Friday, random groups for peer review. No student is "always in the low group."

AI Prompts:

Design Grouping Strategies

I have [number] students in my [grade/subject] class. Based on this deidentified readiness profile: [paste brief profile]

Suggest:

  • How to group students for targeted instruction on [specific skill/concept]
  • What each group should work on to move them forward
  • How long students should stay in these groups before regrouping based on progress

Remember: groups should be temporary and task-specific, not permanent.

Create Interest-Based Groups

Students will be researching [topic]. I know these general interests about my class: [describe interests]

Suggest 4-5 different "interest lenses" for approaching this topic, and how students could self-select into groups based on what fascinates them. All groups should still address the core KUDs, but through their chosen lens.

6

Learning Centres/Stations

▼

Designated classroom areas where students rotate through different activities to explore topics or practice skills at their own pace. Centres allow for simultaneous differentiation—different students working on different things at the same time.

When to use: When you want students to practice skills or explore concepts independently or in small groups. Works well for skill reinforcement, application activities, or exploratory learning.
Example in practice: Four stations for a science unit: (1) hands-on experiment, (2) reading articles and answering questions, (3) creating a diagram/model, (4) digital simulation. Students rotate through all four, or you can assign specific stations based on need.

AI Prompts:

Design Learning Centres

I want to create learning centres for [topic/unit]. My KUDs are: [paste KUDs]

Design 4-5 learning centres that:

  • Address different aspects of the content or skills
  • Use varied modalities (hands-on, reading, digital, creative)
  • Can accommodate different readiness levels within each centre
  • Are engaging and can be completed somewhat independently

For each centre, describe: the activity, materials needed, time required, and how it connects to the KUDs.

Differentiate Within Centres

I have this learning centre activity: [describe activity]

How can I build in differentiation so that students at different readiness levels can all use this centre productively? Suggest:

  • Task cards at different levels of complexity
  • Choice elements that let students select their challenge level
  • Scaffolds available for students who need them
8

Cubing

▼

A strategy where students analyse a topic from six different perspectives (like the six sides of a cube): describe it, compare it, associate it, analyse it, apply it, argue for/against it. You can create different cubes for different readiness levels.

When to use: When you want students to explore a concept from multiple angles. Cubing works well for review, application, or deeper exploration of content students have already encountered.
Example in practice: Students studying ecosystems might get a cube with prompts like: "Describe a food chain," "Compare rainforest and desert ecosystems," "Associate ecosystems with climate," "Analyse what happens when a species is removed," "Apply ecosystem concepts to your local area," "Argue whether humans should intervene in ecosystems."

AI Prompts:

Create Cube Prompts

Create a cubing activity for [topic]. Generate six prompts, one for each side of the cube:

  • Describe: [prompt that asks for description]
  • Compare: [prompt that asks for comparison]
  • Associate: [prompt that asks for connections]
  • Analyse: [prompt that asks for analysis]
  • Apply: [prompt that asks for application]
  • Argue: [prompt that asks for evaluation/position]

Make prompts appropriate for [grade level] and aligned to my KUDs: [paste KUDs]

Create Tiered Cubes

Create two versions of a cube for [topic]:

  • Cube A (Support): More concrete prompts, specific examples, structured thinking
  • Cube B (Extension): More abstract prompts, open-ended thinking, synthesis required

Both cubes should cover the same six perspectives (describe, compare, associate, analyse, apply, argue) but at different levels of complexity.

10

Jigsaw

▼

A cooperative learning strategy where students become "experts" in one aspect of a topic, then teach that aspect to their peers. Students start in "expert groups," then move to "home groups" to share their expertise.

When to use: When a topic has multiple distinct parts that can be learned somewhat independently. Jigsaw promotes interdependence—everyone needs everyone else—and gives students practice teaching.

Example in practice: Teaching about different biomes. Expert groups each research one biome (rainforest, desert, tundra, etc.). Then home groups form with one expert from each biome, and they teach each other, ensuring everyone learns about all biomes.

AI Prompts:

Design a Jigsaw Activity

I want to use jigsaw to teach [topic]. Break this topic into 4-5 distinct parts that:

  • Can be learned somewhat independently
  • Are roughly equal in complexity and importance
  • Connect together to form a complete understanding

For each part, suggest:

  • What the "expert group" should learn
  • Key resources or materials they'll need
  • How they should teach their home group (structure/method)

Differentiate Jigsaw by Readiness

I'm using jigsaw for [topic] with these sections: [list sections]

Some sections are more complex than others. Suggest:

  • Which sections require more sophisticated reading/thinking
  • How to strategically assign sections based on student readiness
  • What scaffolds to provide for students working with more challenging sections

The goal is appropriate challenge for everyone, not just giving "easy" sections to struggling students.

12

Learning Contracts

▼

Formal agreements between teacher and student where the teacher grants freedom in how class time is used in exchange for the student completing individualized skill or content components. Contracts outline what must be done, by when, and to what standard.

When to use: When students are ready for more independence and self-direction. Works well for extensions, independent projects, or when students need to work on specific skills at their own pace.
Example in practice: A student who has mastered core content signs a contract to complete an independent research project. The contract specifies: topic, required elements, check-in dates, assessment criteria, and what the student will do during class time.

AI Prompts:

Create a Learning Contract Template

Create a learning contract template for [grade level] students working on [type of work - e.g., independent project, skill mastery, extension activity].

Include sections for:

  • Learning goals (what student will accomplish)
  • Required components (what must be included)
  • Timeline with check-in dates
  • Quality criteria (how work will be assessed)
  • Student and teacher signatures/commitment

Make it student-friendly but clear and specific.

Design a Personalised Contract

A student has demonstrated mastery of [skill/content] and is ready for extension. Design a learning contract for an independent project where they:

  • Explore [specific extension topic]
  • Demonstrate learning through [product type]
  • Work over [timeframe]

What should the contract specify? What are the non-negotiables vs. what can the student have choice about?

14

Interactive Journals

▼

Journals where students respond to varied writing prompts based on their readiness level. Students reflect on and "own" ideas from their reading and learning. Teacher provides feedback, creating a dialogue.

When to use: When you want students to process and personalise content through writing. Works well for ongoing reflection, connecting new learning to prior knowledge, or exploring concepts more deeply.
Example in practice: After reading a text, students respond to one of three prompts: (1) Summarise the main idea and give one example, (2) Explain how this connects to what we learned last week, (3) Evaluate the author's argument and explain whether you agree or disagree.

AI Prompts:

Create Tiered Journal Prompts

Students just finished [learning activity/reading]. Create three journal prompts at different levels:

  • Level 1: Recall and summarise (What did you learn?)
  • Level 2: Connect and explain (How does this relate to...?)
  • Level 3: Evaluate and create (What's your position on...? What new questions does this raise?)

All prompts should help students process the same core content, just at different cognitive levels.

16

Anchor Activities

▼

Self-directed tasks that students move to automatically upon completing assigned work. Anchor activities manage "ragged time" in a differentiated classroom where students finish at different rates.

When to use: Always. In any differentiated classroom, students will finish tasks at different times. Anchor activities prevent the "I'm done, what do I do now?" problem and keep learning happening for everyone.
Example in practice: Established anchor activities include: independent reading, working on ongoing projects, practicing skills on Khan Academy, journal writing, or peer tutoring. Students know these are always available when they finish other work.

AI Prompts:

Design Anchor Activity Menu

Create a menu of 5-7 anchor activities for my [grade/subject] classroom. These should:

  • Be self-directed (students can do them independently)
  • Be meaningful (not just "busy work")
  • Connect to our current learning or build ongoing skills
  • Accommodate different readiness levels
  • Be sustainable (can be used repeatedly throughout the year)

For each activity, explain how it works and what students gain from it.

18

Predictive Misconception Mapping

▼

Before teaching a differentiated lesson, use AI to predict which parts of each tiered task will cause the most confusion, what specific misconceptions students are likely to develop, and then pre-write a short, targeted reteaching script for each. This shifts misconception response from reactive (assess → discover → improvise) to proactive (predict → prepare → deploy).

When to use: After creating tiered activities, scaffolded tasks, or any differentiated resource — before teaching. Especially valuable when teaching conceptually tricky content, when you're new to a topic or year level, or when you want to feel more prepared for the "what if they don't get it?" moments.
Example in practice: A Year 5 maths lesson on equivalent fractions has three tiered tasks. The teacher asks AI to predict confusion points for each tier. The AI identifies that the support group will likely think ½ and 2/4 are different fractions because "the numbers are different," while the extension group may incorrectly generalise that "multiplying both numbers always works" without understanding why. The teacher receives a 2-minute reteach script for each, ready to deploy at the moment of confusion.

AI Prompts:

Predict Confusion Points Across Tiers

Context: I've created differentiated tasks and I want to anticipate where students will struggle so I'm not caught off guard during the lesson.

Objective: Identify the most likely confusion points, misconceptions, or sticking points for each tier of a differentiated task — before teaching.

Instructional nuance: Different tiers produce different misconceptions. The support group's struggles are often procedural or vocabulary-based; the extension group's are often about overgeneralisation or incomplete conceptual understanding. Anticipating both makes your differentiation more responsive.


Prompt:

I'm teaching [topic] to [Year Level]. Here are my differentiated tasks:

Support tier: [paste]

Core tier: [paste]

Extension tier: [paste]

For each tier, please:

  • Predict the single most likely point of confusion or misconception
  • Explain why students at that level are likely to make this error (what's the underlying thinking?)
  • Suggest one quick diagnostic question I can ask to check whether this misconception is present (something I can ask verbally during the lesson)

Be specific to this content — I don't want generic advice. I want to know exactly where these tasks will cause problems for this age group.

Write a 2-Minute Reteach Script

Context: I know where students are likely to get confused (from the prediction above or from my own experience). I want a short, targeted reteaching script ready to deploy at the moment of confusion.

Objective: Create a brief, teacher-facing reteach script that addresses a specific misconception in 2 minutes or less.

Instructional nuance: A reteach is not "say it again louder." It's a different angle — a new representation, a concrete example, or a targeted question sequence that helps students see their own error. The best reteaches make students think, not just listen.


Prompt:

My [Year Level] students are working on [topic]. The likely misconception is: [describe the specific misconception]

Please write a 2-minute reteaching script I can use at the point of confusion. The script should:

  • Start with a question that surfaces the misconception (don't tell them they're wrong — help them discover it)
  • Use a concrete example, visual representation, or analogy that addresses the root cause
  • End with a "check for understanding" question I can ask to confirm the misconception has been addressed
  • Be written as teacher dialogue — what I actually say and do, step by step

Keep it under 2 minutes of speaking time. This needs to be a quick, surgical intervention, not a mini-lesson.

Create a Misconception Anticipation Map

Context: I'm planning a unit and want to map out common misconceptions across the whole sequence so I can build in prevention strategies, not just responses.

Objective: Create a visual overview of likely misconceptions at each stage of a unit, with prevention and response strategies.

Instructional nuance: Some misconceptions can be prevented through careful sequencing and language choices. Others will emerge no matter what. Knowing the difference helps you plan more strategically.


Prompt:

I'm planning a unit on [topic] for [Year Level]. The key concepts are: [list main concepts in teaching order]

Please create a misconception anticipation map that includes:

  • For each key concept: the 2-3 most common misconceptions students at this level develop
  • Whether each misconception is preventable (through careful teaching) or predictable (likely to emerge regardless)
  • For preventable misconceptions: what specific language, sequencing, or representation choices reduce the risk
  • For predictable misconceptions: a brief reteach approach

Format as a table with columns: Concept | Common Misconception | Preventable or Predictable | Prevention/Response Strategy
