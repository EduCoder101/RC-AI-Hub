🎛️ The Equaliser: Adjusting Challenge to Match Readiness

The music analogy: Like an audio equaliser that adjusts different frequency bands to create the perfect sound, "The Equaliser" helps you adjust different dimensions of a task to create the perfect level of challenge for each student.

"The Equaliser" is a tool designed to help teachers plan differentiated lessons by adjusting "buttons" across several continuums to match student readiness. The goal is to equalise a student's chances of being appropriately challenged—not too easy, not impossibly hard.

Think of these as eight different "knobs" you can turn to make a task more or less complex. You don't need to adjust all eight for every lesson—choose the ones that matter most for your specific content and students.

The Eight Continuums

Click on each continuum below to explore what it means and see examples. Each includes AI prompts to help you adjust tasks along that dimension.

1. Foundational ↔ Transformational

▼
Foundational
Transformational

What this means:

  • Foundational: When an idea is new, students need supporting information that is clear and plainly worded, and time to practice applying the idea in a straightforward way.
  • Transformational: When students are already clear on a concept, they need to move quickly into information that shows them intricacies, stretch and bend the idea, and see how it interacts with other ideas to create new thought.
Example:
  • Foundational task: Classify animals by body covering (fur, scales, feathers)
  • Transformational task: Predict how changes in environment would affect body coverings over time—connect to adaptation and evolution

AI Prompts:

Move Task from Foundational to Transformational:

Here's a foundational task I use: [describe task]

Transform this into a more advanced version that:

  • Assumes students grasp the basics
  • Asks them to explore nuances, complexities, or exceptions
  • Requires synthesis with other concepts
  • Challenges them to see the "big picture" or generate new applications

2. Concrete ↔ Abstract

▼
Concrete
Abstract

What this means:

  • Concrete: Students need to become familiar with key information before looking at deeper meanings. Focus on tangible examples, specific details, and surface-level understanding.
  • Abstract: Once information is grasped concretely, students move to meanings, implications, themes, and underlying principles.
Example (literature):
  • Concrete task: Describe what happens in the story (plot summary)
  • Abstract task: Analyse the story's theme and what the author is saying about human nature

AI Prompts:

Create Concrete and Abstract Versions:

Take this task: [describe task]

Create two versions:

  • Concrete version: Focuses on specific, tangible information and examples. "What" questions.
  • Abstract version: Focuses on themes, implications, underlying principles. "Why" and "what does this mean" questions.

Both should address the same core content but at different levels of abstraction.

3. Simple ↔ Complex

▼
Simple
Complex

What this means:

  • Simple: Students see just the "big picture" or the "skeleton" of a topic without many details. One concept at a time.
  • Complex: Once the skeleton is clear, students add "muscle, bone, and nerves"—multiple details, variables, or concepts simultaneously.
Example (science):
  • Simple task: Identify one variable that affects plant growth
  • Complex task: Investigate how multiple variables (water, light, soil type, temperature) interact to affect plant growth

AI Prompts:

Adjust Complexity Level:

Take this task: [describe task]

Create three versions at different complexity levels:

  • Simple: One main concept/variable, minimal details
  • Moderate: 2-3 concepts/variables, some interconnections
  • Complex: Multiple concepts/variables, numerous details, intricate relationships

Ensure all versions assess the same core understanding but with different levels of complexity.

4. Single Facet ↔ Multiple Facets

▼
Single Facet
Multiple Facets

What this means:

  • Single Facet: Tasks involve only a few steps, one solution method, or focus on one discipline/perspective.
  • Multiple Facets: Tasks require following complicated directions, solving problems that need flexible approaches, or making connections across disciplines.
Example (maths):
  • Single facet task: Solve for x in a linear equation using the standard algorithm
  • Multiple facets task: Solve a real-world problem that requires creating equations, graphing, interpreting results, and explaining why your method works

AI Prompts:

Increase Number of Facets:

Here's a single-facet task: [describe task]

Redesign it to incorporate multiple facets by:

  • Adding steps or stages that require different skills
  • Requiring multiple solution approaches or perspectives
  • Connecting to other disciplines or contexts
  • Asking students to justify or explain their process

The core learning goal remains the same, but the path to it becomes more multifaceted.

5. Small Leap ↔ Great Leap

▼
Small Leap
Great Leap

What this means:

  • Small Leap: Students apply learning in straightforward, similar contexts. Limited transfer required.
  • Great Leap: Students transfer learning to very different contexts, make non-obvious connections, or synthesise across domains.

Note: There is "no option for 'no leap'" because all students must think and apply ideas—the size of the leap varies.

Example (measurement):
  • Small leap: Learn to calculate area, then verify the area of a hamster house
  • Great leap: Learn to calculate area, then estimate materials needed for a building project and calculate proportional cost implications of increasing the area by 50%

AI Prompts:

Adjust Size of Application Leap:

Students are learning [concept/skill]. Create three application tasks with different-sized leaps:

  • Small leap: Apply in a very similar context to what was taught
  • Medium leap: Apply in a moderately different context
  • Great leap: Transfer to a quite different context or synthesise with other learning

All three should require genuine thinking, but the transfer distance varies.

6. Structured ↔ Open-Ended

▼
Structured
Open-Ended

What this means:

  • Structured: Tasks are "fairly well laid out"—like a driver on a prescribed driving course. Clear steps, specific requirements, often "right" answers.
  • Open-Ended: Once modeling has served its purpose, students "branch out and get creative"—craft their own essays, design their own experiments, pose their own questions.
Example (writing):
  • Structured task: Write a paragraph following this exact structure: topic sentence, three supporting details with evidence, concluding sentence
  • Open-ended task: Write a persuasive piece on a topic of your choice, using strategies we've studied—you decide structure and approach

AI Prompts:

Adjust Level of Structure:

Take this task: [describe task]

Create two versions:

  • Structured version: Provide step-by-step directions, templates, models, specific requirements. Students follow a clear path.
  • Open-ended version: Provide the goal and success criteria, but let students determine their approach, structure, and process.

Both should result in the same depth of learning but with different levels of independence.

7. Dependent ↔ Independent

▼
Dependent
Independent

What this means: This continuum tracks students through four specific stages of growing independence:

  1. Skill Building: Developing ability to make simple choices and follow directions
  2. Structured Independence: Making choices from teacher-generated options and following prescribed timelines
  3. Shared Independence: Students generate problems to be solved while teacher helps "tighten" or focus the plans
  4. Self-Guided Independence: Students plan, execute, and evaluate their own tasks, seeking feedback only when needed
Example (research project):
  • Dependent: Teacher provides topic, resources, timeline, and format
  • Structured independence: Student chooses from 5 teacher-provided topics, follows given timeline
  • Shared independence: Student proposes topic and plan, teacher provides feedback and guidance
  • Self-guided independence: Student designs entire project, manages timeline, seeks help as needed

AI Prompts:

Scaffold for Independence:

Students are working on [project/task]. Design scaffolds that gradually release responsibility:

  • Stage 1 (Skill Building): What basic skills do students need? What direct instruction and modeling?
  • Stage 2 (Structured Independence): What choices can students make from options you provide?
  • Stage 3 (Shared Independence): How can students generate ideas while you provide feedback?
  • Stage 4 (Self-Guided): What does full independence look like? How do students know when they're on track?

Not all students will reach Stage 4 on this task—meet them where they are.

8. Slow ↔ Fast

▼
Slow
Fast

What this means:

  • Slow pace: Students need extended time to process, practice, and consolidate learning
  • Fast pace: Students grasp concepts quickly and are ready to move forward

Note: This continuum is "most likely to require jumping around"—students might work slowly on core ideas but quickly through tangential areas, or vice versa.

Adjusting pace thoughtfully:
  • A struggling learner might work slowly at first on core concepts, but then move quickly through supplementary examples once the pattern clicks
  • An advanced learner might move quickly through mechanics but need time to grapple with complex applications

AI Prompts:

Adjust Pacing for Different Learners:

For [topic/unit], design two pacing approaches:

  • Extended pace: Students who need more time—what's the sequence, where do they spend more time, what gets extra practice?
  • Accelerated pace: Students who grasp quickly—what can be compacted, what can move faster, where do they spend time going deeper rather than longer?

Both should achieve the same KUDs: [paste KUDs]. Time allocation is different, not the learning goals.

Using The Equalizer in Practice

You don't need to adjust all eight continuums for every lesson—that would be overwhelming. Instead:

  • Identify which 2-3 continuums are most relevant to your content and students
  • Consider where your students fall on those continuums
  • Slide the "buttons" to create appropriate challenge
  • The result: tasks that meet students where they are and stretch them appropriately

Why The Equalizer Matters

The Equalizer gives you precise language and tools for adjusting challenge. Instead of vaguely thinking "I need to make this harder/easier," you can identify exactly which dimensions to adjust.

This precision ensures that when you differentiate, you're genuinely matching task to student readiness—not just creating "more" or "less" work, but creating the right kind of challenge.

"Appropriate challenge means students are working on something they can't quite do yet—but with effort and support, they can succeed."
— Carol Ann Tomlinson
