Differentiation Without Data is Just Guessing

You can't differentiate effectively without knowing your students. This step is about gathering comprehensive evidence (multiple data points over time) rather than relying on a single assessment.

The goal: Understand where each student is in relation to the learning destination (Step 1), what ignites their curiosity, and how they learn best.

Three Dimensions of Knowing Your Learners

R Readiness

A student's current proximity to the learning goal. This is not about "smart" vs. "not smart." It's about where they are right now in relation to this specific content.

Questions to ask:
  • What prerequisite knowledge or skills do they already have?
  • What misconceptions might they bring?
  • How much support will they need to access this content?
  • Are they ready for extension beyond the core learning?
💡 Key insight: Readiness is content-specific. A student might be advanced in reading but need support in maths, or vice versa.

I Interests

What topics ignite a "spark" or "fire" of curiosity in the student. Interest-based differentiation increases engagement and motivation.

Questions to ask:
  • What topics do they talk about outside of class?
  • What connections can you make between curriculum content and their passions?
  • What examples or contexts would make this content more relevant to them?
💡 Key insight: You don't need to know everything about every student's interests. Even a few key connections can make differentiation more engaging.

P Learning Profile

How a student learns best: their preferred learning style, intelligence preference, cultural background, and environmental needs.

Considerations include:
  • Learning style: Visual, auditory, kinesthetic preferences
  • Intelligence preference: Verbal, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, etc.
  • Working environment: Quiet vs. collaborative, structured vs. flexible
  • Cultural and linguistic background: How their background shapes learning preferences
💡 Key insight: Learning profiles are about preferences, not limitations. Students can learn in multiple ways, but they often have a preference that makes learning more efficient.

Using AI to Know Your Learners

AI can help you create pre-assessments, analyse student data, and build teaching strategies based on what you know about your class.

💡 Remember: Context is Key

For AI prompts to work at their best, provide context about your students and teaching situation. Include details like:

Year level, student ages, class size, prior learning, learning styles present in your class, specific needs or challenges, your curriculum requirements.

The more context you provide, the more tailored and useful the AI's suggestions will be.

Create a Pre-Assessment

▼

I'm about to teach [topic/unit] to [grade level] students. My KUDs are:

  • KNOW: [paste your facts/vocabulary]
  • UNDERSTAND: [paste your concepts]
  • DO: [paste your skills]

Create a pre-assessment that will help me determine:

  • What students already know about this topic
  • What misconceptions they might have
  • What prerequisite skills they have or lack
  • Who might need support vs. who is ready for extension

The assessment should take no more than [10-15 minutes] and use a variety of question types (multiple choice, short answer, visual responses).

Build a Deidentified Class Profile

▼

I teach [grade level] [subject]. Here's a deidentified profile of my students' readiness levels based on recent assessments:

  • Student A: [brief description of readiness]
  • Student B: [brief description]
  • Student C: [brief description]
  • [Continue for class...]

Based on this profile, suggest:

  • How I might group students for differentiated instruction on [topic]
  • What different entry points or scaffolds different groups might need
  • What extension opportunities would challenge students who are ready
💡 Privacy note: When using Enterprise Gemini or similar tools, you can be more flexible with student information, but avoid using full names. Use "Student A," "Student B," or initials instead.

Discover Student Interests

▼

I'm teaching [topic] and want to make connections to student interests. Based on [brief description of your students' ages, backgrounds, common interests], suggest:

  • Real-world connections or examples that would resonate
  • Current events or pop culture references that relate to this content
  • Ways to frame the topic through different interest lenses (sports, music, gaming, art, etc.)

Give me 5-7 concrete ideas I could use to differentiate by interest.

Identify Misconceptions

▼

I'm teaching [topic] to [grade level] students. What are the most common misconceptions students have about this topic?

For each misconception, explain:

  • Why students typically develop this misconception
  • How I can pre-assess for it
  • What instructional strategy would help correct it

Differentiate for Learning Profiles

▼

I have students with varied learning profiles in my [grade level] class. For the topic of [topic], create three versions of the same core activity:

  • Version 1: Optimised for visual learners (use diagrams, graphic organisers, colour coding)
  • Version 2: Optimised for auditory learners (use discussion, oral explanation, listening tasks)
  • Version 3: Optimised for kinesthetic learners (use movement, hands-on manipulation, building/creating)

All three versions should address the same KUDs but engage students differently based on their learning preferences.

Analyse Pre-Assessment Results

▼

I gave my students a pre-assessment on [topic]. Here are the results [paste deidentified data or summary].

Based on this data, help me:

  • Identify which students are ready for the core instruction vs. need review vs. ready for extension
  • Spot patterns in misconceptions or gaps
  • Suggest how I should group students for differentiated instruction
  • Recommend what scaffolding or supports different groups will need

Why This Step Matters

Effective differentiation is responsive, not arbitrary. When you differentiate based on actual student data (readiness, interests, and learning profiles), you create learning experiences that genuinely meet students where they are.

Without this step, you're essentially creating different versions of content based on assumptions. With this step, you're creating different versions based on evidence.

"Differentiation is not something we do to students. It is something we do for students, based on what we know about them."
- Carol Ann Tomlinson
