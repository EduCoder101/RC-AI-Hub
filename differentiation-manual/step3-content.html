Theory: Why Content Differentiation Comes First

Click to expand research foundations

▼

Research consistently shows that differentiating content—how students access information—is the most powerful way to ensure equitable learning opportunities.

When students can't access the content because it's too complex, too abstract, or presented in an inaccessible format, no amount of process or product differentiation will help. Content access is the foundation.

Content refers to the "input"—what students learn and how they gain access to it.

Tiered Texts & Resource Materials

▼

Provide reading materials at different complexity and readability levels so students can engage with the same overarching theme or concept regardless of their current literacy skills.

When to use: When the core content involves significant reading, and you have students at varied reading levels. This ensures all students can access the concepts without being blocked by text complexity.
Example in practice: Three versions of an article about climate change—one at grade level, one simplified (without losing conceptual depth), and one with added complexity for advanced readers.

AI Prompts:

Create Tiered Versions of a Text

Here is a text I'm using with my [grade level] class: [paste text]

Create three versions of this text:

  • Version 1 (Support): Simplified vocabulary and sentence structure for students reading below grade level, but maintain the conceptual integrity. Do not "dumb down" the ideas—make them more accessible.
  • Version 2 (Core): Appropriate for grade-level readers.
  • Version 3 (Extension): Increased complexity, academic vocabulary, and additional nuance for advanced readers.

Ensure all three versions convey the same core concepts and maintain similar length (±20%).

Assess Text Readability

Analyse this text for readability: [paste text]

Tell me:

  • Approximate reading level (grade equivalent)
  • What makes it challenging (vocabulary, sentence structure, concept density)
  • Which students in my [grade level] class might struggle with it
  • What specific adaptations would make it more accessible without losing rigor

Multi-Modal Presentations

▼

Deliver information through multiple modes—auditory (recordings, discussions), visual (graphic organisers, diagrams, videos), and tactile/kinesthetic (hands-on models, movement).

When to use: When students have varied learning profiles or when content is complex and benefits from multiple representations. This is especially powerful for EALD students or students with learning differences.
Example in practice: Teaching the water cycle through a written explanation, a video animation, a physical model students can manipulate, and a kinesthetic activity where students "become" water molecules moving through the cycle.

AI Prompts:

Create Multi-Modal Versions

I'm teaching [topic] to [grade level] students. Currently, my instruction is primarily [text-based/lecture-based/etc.].

Help me create multi-modal versions that present the same core content through:

  • Visual mode: Infographic, diagram, graphic organiser, or visual sequence
  • Auditory mode: Script for a podcast-style explanation or discussion prompts
  • Kinesthetic mode: Hands-on activity, physical model, or movement-based learning

All modes should cover the same KUDs but engage students through their preferred learning channel.

Transform Text into Visual Representation

Take this written content and transform it into a visual representation: [paste text]

Create either:

  • A graphic organiser that shows relationships between concepts
  • An infographic that presents the information visually
  • A flowchart or diagram that sequences the content

Provide the structure and labels—I'll format it for students.

Curriculum Compacting

▼

A three-stage process: (1) identify what students have already mastered through pre-assessment, (2) eliminate redundant practice for those students, (3) replace it with meaningful curriculum extensions or independent projects.

When to use: When pre-assessment reveals that some students have already mastered portions of the content. This prevents boredom and allows advanced learners to move into deeper exploration.
Example in practice: A student demonstrates mastery of multiplication facts through pre-assessment, so instead of drilling facts they already know, they explore patterns in multiplication, investigate the relationship to division, or apply multiplication to real-world problem-solving.

AI Prompts:

Design a Compacting Plan

Based on pre-assessment, several students in my [grade level] class have already mastered [specific content/skills].

Help me design a curriculum compacting plan:

  • Stage 1: What evidence would confirm mastery? (Design a brief assessment)
  • Stage 2: What portions of the standard curriculum can be eliminated for these students?
  • Stage 3: What extensions or enrichment activities would deepen their learning on this topic? Provide 3-4 options that go beyond "more of the same."

Digest of Key Ideas

▼

Create a one- or two-page "capsule" of essential questions, vocabulary, and concepts to support students who struggle with heavy print materials or organisation.

When to use: When students are overwhelmed by lengthy texts or complex materials. This gives them a roadmap and helps them focus on what matters most.
Example in practice: Before reading a chapter in a history textbook, provide students with a one-page digest that outlines: the essential question, key vocabulary with definitions, main concepts to watch for, and 3-4 guiding questions.

AI Prompts:

Create a Content Digest

Students will be reading this text/chapter: [paste text or describe content]

Create a one-page digest that includes:

  • Essential Question: The big question this content answers
  • Key Vocabulary: 5-8 terms with student-friendly definitions
  • Main Concepts: The 3-4 core ideas students must grasp
  • Guiding Questions: 4-5 questions to focus reading

Format it so students can use it as a reference while they read.

Highlighted Print Materials

▼

Highlight critical passages in a text so that struggling readers can focus energy on essential portions rather than becoming discouraged by an overwhelming amount of print.

When to use: When a lengthy text contains essential content, but not every paragraph is equally important. This helps students with reading challenges or executive functioning difficulties know where to focus.
Example in practice: A 5-page article where pages 2 and 4 contain the core information. Highlight those sections for students who need support, with a note: "Start here—these are the most important paragraphs."

AI Prompts:

Identify Key Passages

Here is a text my students need to read: [paste text]

Help me identify:

  • Which paragraphs or passages contain the essential information aligned to my KUDs
  • Which sections are supplementary or provide examples but aren't critical
  • A suggested highlighting strategy for students who need support (e.g., "Read highlighted sections first, then return to read the full text if time permits")

Support Systems (Buddies, Mentors, Technology)

▼

Use reading buddies, adult mentors, or audio/video recordings to make complex content accessible to all students.

When to use: When content is challenging but essential, and students need human or technological support to access it. This is particularly effective for EALD students or students with reading difficulties.
Example in practice: Pair a strong reader with a struggling reader to read a text together, or provide an audio recording of the text so students can listen while they read.

AI Prompts:

Create an Audio Script

I want to create an audio version of this text for students who benefit from listening: [paste text]

Rewrite it as a script that would work well as an audio recording, including:

  • Natural speaking pauses
  • Verbal cues for emphasis ("This next point is really important...")
  • Brief summaries at logical breaks

Keep the core content intact but make it sound like spoken language, not written text.

Design a Reading Buddy Protocol

I want to implement reading buddies for [topic/text]. Design a protocol that:

  • Structures how pairs should read together (alternating paragraphs, echo reading, etc.)
  • Includes prompts for discussion after each section
  • Assigns roles so both students are actively engaged
  • Ensures the stronger reader is supporting, not just "doing it for" the other student

Visual Supports: Glossaries, Mind Maps, Flashcards

▼

Provide visual tools that help students organise, remember, and connect content. These can be pre-made or co-created with students.

When to use: When content is vocabulary-heavy, conceptually dense, or requires students to see relationships between ideas. Visual supports are especially helpful for visual learners and students who struggle with working memory.
Example in practice: A mind map showing how different concepts in a science unit connect, with vocabulary flashcards for key terms, and a visual glossary with pictures alongside definitions.

AI Prompts:

Create a Visual Glossary

Create a visual glossary for these key terms from my [topic] unit: [list terms]

For each term, provide:

  • Student-friendly definition (1-2 sentences)
  • A visual description or analogy that would help students remember it
  • An example of how the term is used in context

Format it so I can turn it into a visual reference sheet.

Design a Mind Map

Create a mind map structure for [topic] that shows how these concepts connect: [list concepts]

Organise it hierarchically with:

  • The central concept in the middle
  • Major branches for main ideas
  • Sub-branches for supporting details
  • Visual cues about which concepts relate to each other

Describe the structure so I can create it visually for students.

Neurodiversity-Responsive Content Design

▼

Design content that is accessible for neurodivergent learners by reducing cognitive load, providing multimodal formats, using concrete language, and offering strength-based supports. This approach benefits all students while specifically supporting those with ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, dyslexia, and executive functioning challenges.

When to use: When you have students with diagnosed learning differences, when content involves complex language or multi-step processes, or when you want to make content universally accessible from the start.
Example in practice: A task sheet that combines written instructions with simple icons, uses colour coding to distinguish different sections, breaks tasks into numbered steps, and avoids metaphorical language like "dive into" or "tackle the problem."

AI Prompts:

Design Low-Demand Version Maintaining Core Intent

Context: I have a task that requires significant cognitive load, and I need a version that reduces demands without watering down the learning goal.

Objective: Create a streamlined version that maintains the essential learning while reducing extraneous cognitive load.

Instructional nuance: This isn't about making it "easier"—it's about removing unnecessary complexity so students can focus on the core skill or concept. Maintain rigor, reduce load.


Prompt:

Here is my original task: [paste full task description]

The core learning goal is: [state the essential KUD]

Please create a low-demand version that:

  • Maintains the same learning goal
  • Reduces cognitive load by removing unnecessary steps, choices, or information
  • Provides more structure or clearer parameters
  • Makes success criteria more explicit

Identify what you removed and why, so I understand the cognitive load you've reduced.

Convert to Multimodal Format (Text/Visual/Colour)

Context: I have a task sheet that is primarily text-based, and I need to make it more accessible for students who process information better with visual supports.

Objective: Convert the task into a multimodal version using text, simple visuals, and colour coding.

Instructional nuance: Use colour and visuals purposefully, not decoratively. Each element should support comprehension.


Prompt:

Here is my current task sheet: [paste content]

Please redesign it as a multimodal version:

  • Text: Keep key instructions but make them concise and concrete
  • Visuals: Suggest simple icons, diagrams, or symbols to represent key steps or concepts
  • Colour coding: Suggest a colour scheme to help students distinguish between different types of information (e.g., instructions vs. examples), see the sequence or structure, and identify what's most important

Provide:

  • Revised text with [VISUAL] placeholders showing where visuals go
  • Description of each suggested visual
  • Colour coding rationale (e.g., 'Blue = instructions, Green = examples')

Rewrite with Chunked Steps and Concrete Language

Context: My task instructions contain abstract language or long paragraphs that may be difficult for students who need concrete, sequential information.

Objective: Break the task into numbered steps using clear, concrete language.

Instructional nuance: Replace abstract concepts with concrete examples. Use active verbs. Make implicit steps explicit.


Prompt:

Here are my task instructions: [paste instructions]

Please rewrite them as:

  • Numbered steps (no more than 8 steps if possible)
  • Concrete, specific language (avoid metaphors, abstract terms, or implied information)
  • Active verbs that tell students exactly what to do
  • One clear action per step

If any step is still complex, break it into sub-steps (e.g., 3a, 3b).

Also note any abstract language you replaced and why it might have been confusing.

Create Visual Schedule with Icons

Context: I need to help students understand the sequence and timing of activities in a lesson or task.

Objective: Create a visual schedule that shows what will happen, in what order, and approximately how long each part will take.

Instructional nuance: Visual schedules support executive functioning and reduce anxiety by making expectations clear and predictable.


Prompt:

Here is the lesson/task sequence: [describe activities in order]

Please create a visual schedule that includes:

  • Each activity/step with a simple icon or symbol to represent it
  • Estimated time for each part
  • A clear way to show progression (e.g., numbered boxes, timeline)

For each activity, suggest:

  • A simple icon/symbol (describe what it would look like)
  • The activity name in 2-4 words
  • Time allocation

Format it so a teacher could quickly recreate this on a slide, poster, or handout.

Rewrite Avoiding Abstract/Metaphorical Language

Context: My content or instructions use figurative language, metaphors, or abstract concepts that may confuse literal thinkers or neurodivergent students.

Objective: Rewrite using only concrete, literal language while maintaining the meaning.

Instructional nuance: Some students interpret language very literally. Remove idioms, metaphors, and assumed cultural references.


Prompt:

Here is my content/instructions: [paste content]

Please rewrite it to:

  • Remove all metaphorical or figurative language
  • Replace idioms with literal equivalents
  • Use concrete, specific descriptions instead of abstract concepts
  • Remove implied meanings—make everything explicit

Highlight any phrases you changed and explain why the original might have been confusing for literal thinkers.

Suggest Strength-Based Support for Working Memory

Context: I have students with working memory challenges who struggle to hold multiple pieces of information in mind while completing tasks.

Objective: Design supports that reduce working memory demands while affirming student strengths.

Instructional nuance: Frame supports as tools for efficiency, not as accommodations for deficiency. Use a strengths-first lens.


Prompt:

Here is the task: [describe task]

This task requires students to: [list what they need to hold in working memory, e.g., 'remember the question while reading the passage,' 'track multiple steps,' etc.]

Please suggest 3-4 strength-based supports that would reduce working memory demands. For each support:

  • Describe the tool or strategy
  • Explain how it reduces cognitive load
  • Frame it positively (e.g., 'This tool helps you work efficiently' not 'This is for students who can't remember')

Examples of tools: reference sheets, colour-coded reminders, chunking with check-in points, graphic organizers, etc.

Analogy Generator for Concept Accessibility

▼

Use AI to generate multiple analogies for abstract or complex concepts, with each analogy drawn from a different domain (sport, nature, everyday life, technology, cooking, gaming, etc.). This allows you to choose the analogy that best fits your students' interests and experiences, or to offer different analogies to different students as a form of interest-based content differentiation.

When to use: When content involves abstract concepts that students struggle to visualise or connect to their existing knowledge. Particularly effective when introducing new topics, when students have diverse interests you can leverage, or when a concept needs to "click" before students can engage with more complex tasks.
Example in practice: Teaching the concept of "ecosystems and interdependence" in Science. The teacher generates analogies: a football team (each player has a role; remove the goalkeeper and the whole team suffers), a recipe (each ingredient contributes; change one and the result changes), and a school (teachers, students, admin all depend on each other). Students choose the analogy that resonates, then use it to explain the concept in their own words.

AI Prompts:

Generate Multiple Analogies from Different Domains

Context: I need to explain an abstract concept and I want multiple analogies so I can choose the best one for my students — or offer different analogies to different groups.

Objective: Create 4-5 analogies for the same concept, each drawn from a different domain that [Year Level] students would find relatable.

Instructional nuance: A good analogy has a strong structural mapping — the relationships between elements in the analogy should mirror the relationships in the target concept. A bad analogy just has surface similarity. Push for analogies where the logic transfers, not just the vibe.


Prompt:

I need to teach [concept] to [Year Level] students. Many of them find this concept abstract or hard to visualise.

Please generate 5 different analogies to explain this concept, each from a different domain:

  • Sport or physical activity
  • Everyday life (cooking, household, transport)
  • Nature or animals
  • Technology or gaming
  • Social relationships or school life

For each analogy:

  • Explain the analogy in 2-3 sentences, as you'd say it to students
  • Map the key elements: "In this analogy, [X] represents [Y], and [A] represents [B]"
  • Note where the analogy breaks down (all analogies have limits — I need to know where this one stops working so I don't create a new misconception)

Keep language appropriate for [Year Level].

Create an Interest-Differentiated Analogy Activity

Context: I want to use analogies as a differentiation-by-interest strategy, where different students work with different analogies for the same concept.

Objective: Design a short activity where students use their chosen analogy to explain a concept, then compare analogies with peers to deepen understanding.

Instructional nuance: The power of this activity is in the comparison phase. When students see that different analogies illuminate different aspects of the same concept, they develop richer, more flexible understanding. Don't skip the share-back.


Prompt:

I'm teaching [concept] to [Year Level]. I want to create an activity where students choose an analogy that resonates with them and use it to demonstrate understanding.

Using the concept of [concept], please:

  • Provide 4 analogies from different domains (sport, cooking, nature, technology) — each written in student-friendly language
  • Create a brief "Analogy Card" template for each, with:
    • The analogy described in 2-3 sentences
    • A prompt: "How is [analogy situation] like [concept]? Map at least 3 connections"
    • A challenge question: "Where does this analogy stop working? What part of [concept] doesn't fit?"
  • Design a 5-minute share-back protocol where students with different analogies compare their mappings

The Learning Intention is: [paste LI]

Extend an Analogy into a Worked Example

Context: I have an analogy that works well and I want to build a fuller teaching sequence around it — using it as the through-line for a lesson segment.

Objective: Take a single strong analogy and develop it into a structured teaching sequence that moves from the familiar (analogy) to the abstract (concept).

Instructional nuance: The analogy is the entry point, not the destination. Students should move from understanding the analogy → mapping it to the concept → working with the concept directly. If students can only explain the concept through the analogy, the scaffold hasn't faded.


Prompt:

I'm using this analogy to teach [concept] to [Year Level]:

[describe the analogy]

Please build a short teaching sequence (10-15 minutes) that uses this analogy as the through-line:

  • Hook (2 min): How to introduce the analogy in a way that grabs attention
  • Mapping (5 min): Guided questions that help students connect the analogy to the concept — what corresponds to what?
  • Transfer (5 min): An activity where students apply the concept (not just the analogy) to a new situation
  • Check (2 min): A question I can ask to confirm students understand the concept itself, not just the analogy

Write the teacher dialogue for steps 1 and 2 — what I actually say and ask.
