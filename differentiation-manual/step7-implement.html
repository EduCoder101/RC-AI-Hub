Differentiation is Never "Done"

Differentiation is an organic and evolutionary process—not a one-time event. You differentiate, you observe how it's working, you gather data through formative assessment, and you adjust. The cycle repeats continuously.

This step is where differentiation becomes truly responsive. You're using ongoing assessment to guide your next instructional moves, regrouping students as needed, and refining your approach based on what you're learning about student progress.

Why This Step Completes the Cycle

Steps 1-6 are planning. Step 7 is where differentiation becomes truly responsive—where you use real-time data to adjust and refine your approach.

The best differentiation isn't the most elaborate planning. It's the most responsive teaching—the ability to notice what students need and adjust accordingly.

"Good teaching is more about asking the right questions than having all the right answers. It's more about watching students than delivering content."
— Carol Ann Tomlinson

The Cycle Continues

You've now moved through all seven steps of effective differentiation. But remember: this is a cycle, not a checklist.

You'll return to Step 1 with new learning goals. You'll gather new data about students (Step 2). You'll choose new strategies (Steps 3-4). You'll adjust challenge (Step 5), provide scaffolds (Step 6), and continuously assess and respond (Step 7).

Differentiation is never "finished"—it's an ongoing, evolving practice. And with AI as your partner, you now have the capacity to make this sustainable.

Formative Assessment Strategies

2

Exit Cards / Tickets

▼

Brief written responses students complete at the end of a lesson. These provide you with snapshot data about what students understood (or didn't) that you can use to plan tomorrow's instruction.

When to use: End of most lessons, particularly after introducing new concepts or completing key activities.
Effective prompts:
  • 3-2-1: 3 things you learned, 2 questions you have, 1 thing you found surprising
  • One-sentence summary of today's key idea
  • Rate your understanding 1-5 and explain why
  • Apply today's concept to solve this quick problem

AI Prompts:

Design Exit Card Prompts

I just taught [topic/lesson]. Create 3-4 exit card prompts that would:

  • Quickly assess whether students grasped the key concepts
  • Reveal common misconceptions
  • Be answerable in 2-3 minutes
  • Give me actionable data for tomorrow's teaching

KUDs: [paste KUDs]

Analyse Exit Card Data

I asked students this exit card question: [paste question]

Here's a summary of their responses: [paste deidentified data or themes]

Help me interpret this data:

  • What patterns do you see?
  • What misconceptions are evident?
  • Which students seem to need support vs. extension?
  • What should I do in tomorrow's lesson based on this data?
3

QuickWrites

▼

Brief, timed writing activities (2-3 minutes) that ask students to synthesise learning or respond to a prompt. These reveal student thinking and provide formative data.

When to use: Mid-lesson to check understanding before moving on, or to help students process what they've just learned.
Example prompts: "In your own words, explain..." "What's the connection between X and Y?" "How does this relate to what we learned yesterday?"

AI Prompts:

Generate QuickWrite Prompts

Students just finished [activity/discussion]. Create 3 QuickWrite prompts that would help them process and consolidate this learning. Each prompt should:

  • Be answerable in 2-3 minutes
  • Require thinking, not just recall
  • Reveal whether students grasped the core ideas

Topic: [topic], KUDs: [paste KUDs]

4

Think-Pair-Share & Structured Discussion

▼

Give students a moment to think individually, then discuss with a partner, then share with the class. While students talk, you circulate and listen—gathering formative data about understanding.

When to use: Throughout lessons to check understanding, activate prior knowledge, or process new information.
What to listen for: Accurate use of vocabulary, ability to explain concepts, misconceptions, level of confidence, quality of examples

AI Prompts:

Design Discussion Prompts

Create think-pair-share prompts for [topic] that would:

  • Check for understanding at different points in the lesson
  • Be open enough for discussion but focused enough to be productive
  • Reveal student thinking

For each prompt, tell me what I should listen for that would indicate understanding vs. confusion.

5

Dry Erase Boards / Immediate Response

▼

Students show their answers simultaneously on whiteboards (or digital equivalents). You can immediately see who gets it and who doesn't.

When to use: For quick checks during instruction, practicing skills, or gauging readiness before moving on.
Works well for: Math problems, vocabulary definitions, identifying examples, true/false, multiple choice, short answers

AI Prompts:

Create Quick-Check Questions

Generate 8-10 questions I can use with whiteboards to check understanding of [topic]. Questions should:

  • Have clear, demonstrable answers (so I can see who gets it at a glance)
  • Progress from simple to complex
  • Reveal common misconceptions
  • Be answerable in under a minute each
6

Varying Questions by Readiness

▼

During class discussion, target different questions to different students based on their readiness. This ensures all students are held accountable and appropriately challenged.

When to use: During whole-class discussions and questioning.
Example: For a text analysis discussion:
  • Student A (needs support): "What happened in this section?"
  • Student B (on-level): "Why did the character make that choice?"
  • Student C (advanced): "How does the author use this scene to develop the theme?"

AI Prompts:

Generate Tiered Discussion Questions

I'm leading a discussion about [topic]. Create tiered questions at three levels:

  • Level 1: Recall and comprehension (accessible to all)
  • Level 2: Application and analysis (appropriate challenge for most)
  • Level 3: Evaluation and synthesis (extension for advanced thinkers)

Create 3-4 questions at each level, all addressing the core content but at different cognitive levels.

7

S-O-S Summary (Statement-Opinion-Support)

▼

Students write a brief response: State an opinion on a statement (agree/disagree), then support it with evidence. Provides practice for critical thinking without requiring a full essay.

When to use: When you want students to practice argumentation and evidence-based thinking in a low-stakes, quick format.
Structure: "Statement: [controversial or debatable claim]. Your opinion (agree/disagree): ____ Support your opinion with evidence."

AI Prompts:

Create SOS Prompts

Generate 5 SOS statements for [topic] that would:

  • Be debatable (not obvious facts)
  • Connect to core content
  • Require students to use evidence/reasoning
  • Provoke thinking

For each statement, note what correct support might include.

8

Observation Protocols with Rubrics

▼

Use a simple observation rubric to systematically note student progress during activities. A 4-point scale with clear descriptors helps you quickly identify understanding levels without disrupting the flow of teaching.

When to use: During independent work, group activities, or discussions when you want to systematically track who understands and who needs support.
Example: While students work on a problem-solving task, circulate with a simple grid noting each student's level: 1 (struggling), 2 (developing), 3 (proficient), 4 (extending).

AI Prompts:

Create Observation Protocol with 4-Point Scale

Context: I want to observe students during [activity] and collect formative data on their progress with [skill/concept].

Objective: Design a simple observation protocol with clear descriptors for different levels of understanding.

Instructional nuance: The protocol should be quick to use during teaching, not a detailed rubric. Focus on observable behaviours that indicate understanding.


Prompt:

I'm observing students during [describe activity].

The learning goal is: [state what you're assessing]

Please create a 4-point observation scale:

  • Level 1: Not yet demonstrating understanding
  • Level 2: Developing understanding
  • Level 3: Demonstrating understanding
  • Level 4: Extending understanding

For each level, provide:

  • 2-3 specific, observable indicators (What would I see or hear?)
  • A brief example of what a student might say or do

Make it concrete enough that I can quickly identify which level a student is at during the lesson.

9

Quick Feedback Based on Common Responses

▼

Anticipate typical student responses and prepare targeted feedback ahead of time. This allows you to give specific, actionable feedback quickly when you're circulating or reviewing student work.

When to use: When planning a task or question where you can predict common correct, partially correct, and incorrect responses.
Example: For a maths problem, prepare feedback for: correct solution, correct answer with flawed reasoning, common computational error, and conceptual misunderstanding.

AI Prompts:

Generate Quick Feedback Suggestions Based on Common Responses

Context: I've collected student responses to [task/question] and I want to provide quick, targeted feedback that moves learning forward.

Objective: Anticipate common responses and generate feedback that is specific, actionable, and developmental.

Instructional nuance: Good feedback is timely, specific, and focused on the learning goal—not just "good job" or "try again."


Prompt:

I asked students: [paste your question or task]

The learning goal was: [state what skill or understanding you're targeting]

Please:

  • Anticipate 3-4 common types of student responses (e.g., correct, partially correct with specific gap, common misconception, off-track)
  • For each response type, suggest quick feedback I could give that:
    • Acknowledges what the student has done
    • Identifies the specific next step or thinking move
    • Keeps the cognitive work with the student (doesn't just give the answer)

Format as: Response type → Suggested feedback

10

Peer Assessment Checklists

▼

Scaffold peer feedback with simple "Got it / Need help" checklists focused on success criteria. This builds students' assessment literacy while providing you with multiple data points on student work.

When to use: When students can meaningfully assess each other's work against clear criteria, particularly for drafts or work-in-progress.
Example: For persuasive writing: ✓ Clear thesis statement (Got it / Need help), ✓ Three pieces of evidence (Got it / Need help), ✓ Addresses counterargument (Got it / Need help)

AI Prompts:

Design Peer Assessment Checklist

Context: Students will be giving each other feedback on [task/product] and I want to scaffold this with a simple checklist.

Objective: Create a peer assessment tool that is simple, specific, and focused on the most important success criteria.

Instructional nuance: Peer assessment works best when criteria are clear, observable, and limited in number. Use "Got it / Need help" rather than scores or grades.


Prompt:

Students are working on: [describe the task or product]

The key success criteria are: [list 3-5 most important criteria]

Please create a peer assessment checklist with:

  • 4-6 specific, observable criteria
  • For each criterion, two columns: 'Got it' and 'Need help'
  • A sentence prompt for each criterion (e.g., 'The introduction clearly states the main argument')
  • A final section: 'One thing done really well' and 'One suggestion for improvement'

Keep the language simple and concrete so students can use it independently.

11

Digital Exit Tickets (Google Forms)

▼

Use Google Forms for quick digital exit tickets that automatically compile student responses into a spreadsheet, making data analysis faster and more efficient.

When to use: When you want to quickly gather and analyse exit ticket data, particularly if students have devices available.
Benefits: Instant data compilation, easy to see patterns, can include images or diagrams, allows for multiple question types, students can't lose their paper ticket

AI Prompts:

Create Google Form Exit Ticket

Context: I want to check understanding at the end of my lesson using a quick Google Form that students can complete in 2-3 minutes.

Objective: Design 3-4 exit ticket questions that give me actionable data on student understanding.

Instructional nuance: Mix question types (multiple choice for quick data, short answer for deeper insight). Questions should directly assess the lesson's learning intention.


Prompt:

I just taught: [lesson topic]

The learning intention was: [state what students should know/understand/be able to do]

Please design a 3-4 question exit ticket for Google Forms:

  • Question 1: One multiple choice question (with 4 options) that checks factual understanding
  • Question 2: One short answer question that reveals thinking or application
  • Question 3: One self-assessment question (e.g., 'How confident do you feel about [skill]?')

For each question, provide:

  • The question text
  • For multiple choice: all four options (including plausible distractors)
  • The rationale: What will this question tell me about student learning?

Keep it focused so students can complete it in 2-3 minutes.

12

Bias & Cultural Assumption Audit

▼

Before using any AI-generated differentiated material in the classroom, prompt the AI to critically examine the resource for unspoken cultural assumptions, socioeconomic bias, or representation gaps. This is a quality assurance step that catches issues teachers may not notice because the assumptions feel "normal" within their own context.

When to use: After generating any differentiated resource — tiered texts, choice boards, scaffolded activities, assessment tasks. Particularly important when creating materials for diverse classrooms, when content touches on cultural topics, or when tasks reference home environments, family structures, or out-of-school activities.
Example in practice: A teacher generates a tiered reading task about Australian ecosystems. The audit reveals that all three tiers assume students have visited a beach or national park, use idioms like "fair go" that newly arrived students may not understand, and reference "asking Mum and Dad" in the homework extension — excluding students in different family structures. The teacher revises accordingly.

AI Prompts:

Audit for Cultural & Socioeconomic Assumptions

Context: I've created (or AI has helped me create) a differentiated resource, and I want to check it for hidden assumptions before giving it to students.

Objective: Identify any cultural, socioeconomic, or representational assumptions embedded in the material that could create unintended barriers or exclusions.

Instructional nuance: This isn't about being "politically correct" — it's about ensuring that the resource genuinely works for every student in your classroom. Hidden assumptions are the most common reason differentiated materials fail for the students they're supposed to help most.


Prompt:

Here is a differentiated resource I plan to use with my [Year Level] class: [paste full resource]

My class includes [briefly describe diversity — e.g., "students from 12 different cultural backgrounds, 4 EALD students, varied socioeconomic contexts"].

Please audit this resource for:

  • Cultural assumptions: Does it assume specific cultural knowledge, traditions, or experiences? Are examples and references inclusive?
  • Socioeconomic assumptions: Does it assume access to resources, technology, or experiences that not all students may have?
  • Family structure assumptions: Does it reference "parents," "home," or family activities in ways that might exclude some students?
  • Language assumptions: Are there idioms, colloquialisms, or culturally specific phrases that EALD students might not understand?
  • Representation: Do examples, names, and scenarios reflect the diversity of an Australian classroom?

For each issue found, suggest a specific revision.

Check Differentiated Tiers for Respectful Tasks

Context: I've created tiered versions of a task (support, core, extension) and I want to make sure the support tier doesn't feel patronising or "lesser" compared to the others.

Objective: Evaluate whether all tiers of a differentiated task maintain intellectual dignity and engagement — Tomlinson's principle of "respectful tasks."

Instructional nuance: The most common failure in differentiation is creating a support tier that looks different in a way that signals to students they're getting the "easy" or "baby" version. Students notice. This audit catches that problem.


Prompt:

Here are three tiered versions of a task I've created for [topic]:

Support tier: [paste]

Core tier: [paste]

Extension tier: [paste]

Please evaluate these tiers for "respectful tasks" — the principle that every version should feel equally valued and intellectually engaging. Specifically:

  • Would a student receiving the support tier feel their work is meaningful and respected, or would it feel like a "dumbed down" version?
  • Are there visual or structural cues that might signal to students which tier is "lower" (e.g., significantly shorter, childish formatting, less interesting content)?
  • Does the support tier still require genuine thinking, or has it been reduced to fill-in-the-blank busywork?
  • Are all three tiers working toward the same Learning Intention?

Suggest specific revisions to ensure all tiers maintain intellectual dignity.

The Devil's Advocate — AI Self-Critique

▼

After generating differentiated materials, prompt the AI to critically evaluate its own output from a student's perspective. Ask it to identify what a disengaged student would find boring, what a struggling student would find confusing, and what assumptions it made that might be wrong. Then use those insights to revise the resource before it reaches students.

When to use: After creating any differentiated resource — particularly tiered activities, scaffolded tasks, or choice boards. Use as a quality check before finalising materials. Especially valuable when you've been working on a resource for a while and need fresh eyes, or when you're creating for a class profile you're less familiar with.
Example in practice: A teacher creates a tiered writing task on persuasive texts. They ask AI to critique it. The AI identifies that the support tier has been over-scaffolded — it's essentially a fill-in-the-blank exercise that removes all genuine persuasive thinking. The extension tier is "write more paragraphs" rather than requiring deeper reasoning. The AI suggests specific revisions that maintain the scaffolding but restore intellectual challenge.

AI Prompts:

The Disengaged Student Test

Context: I've created a differentiated resource and I want to stress-test it from the perspective of a student who isn't naturally motivated by this topic.

Objective: Identify what would make a disengaged or reluctant learner switch off, and suggest targeted fixes.

Instructional nuance: Disengaged students are often the best detectors of weak pedagogy. If a task can hold the attention of a student who doesn't care about the topic, it's probably a good task. This prompt helps you see your resource through their eyes.


Prompt:

Here is a differentiated resource I've created for [Year Level] on [topic]: [paste full resource]

Please adopt the perspective of a disengaged [Year Level] student — someone who finds this topic boring, would rather be doing something else, and will look for any excuse to check out.

Tell me:

  • At exactly what point would this student mentally switch off? Why?
  • Which instructions would they find confusing, overly wordy, or pointless?
  • What's the "so what?" problem — does the task give students a reason to care?
  • Suggest 2-3 specific, practical changes that would make this more engaging without changing the Learning Intention

Be brutally honest. I'd rather fix problems now than discover them in the classroom.

Critique Your Own Assumptions

Context: AI-generated materials inevitably carry assumptions about what students know, what they find interesting, and what they can do. I want to surface those assumptions explicitly.

Objective: Force the AI to examine and disclose the assumptions it made when generating a differentiated resource, so the teacher can verify or challenge them.

Instructional nuance: AI doesn't flag its own assumptions unless you ask. It will confidently produce a "support" version that assumes students can read at a level they can't, or an "extension" task that assumes background knowledge students don't have. This prompt makes the invisible visible.


Prompt:

Look at the differentiated resource you just helped me create.

Now critique it honestly:

  • What assumptions did you make about students' prior knowledge that might be wrong?
  • What assumptions did you make about reading level, attention span, or working independently?
  • Are there any parts where you defaulted to a generic approach rather than something genuinely tailored to [Year Level]?
  • If you were a teacher looking at this resource for the first time, what would your first concern be?

Don't defend your choices — challenge them. I want to know where this resource might fail in practice.
